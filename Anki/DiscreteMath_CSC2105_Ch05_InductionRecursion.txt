State the 3-part structure of a proof by mathematical induction.	Base case (show P(n0) is true), inductive step (assume P(k) true, prove P(k+1) follows), conclusion (by induction, P(n) holds for all n >= n0)
Why must the inductive step explicitly use the inductive hypothesis?	Because a valid inductive proof must show P(k+1) FOLLOWS FROM the assumption that P(k) is true; not using the assumption means you haven't actually proven the implication, just restated the claim
What distinguishes strong induction from regular induction?	Strong induction assumes P(n0) through P(k) are ALL true (not just P(k)) before proving P(k+1); needed when P(k+1) depends on more than just the immediately preceding case
Are strong induction and regular induction equally powerful?	Yes — they are logically equivalent in power; strong induction is just more convenient when the inductive step needs more than the immediate predecessor
Give the recursive definition of the factorial function.	f(0) = 1 (base case); f(n) = n * f(n-1) for n >= 1 (recursive case)
Give the recursive definition of the Fibonacci sequence, including base cases.	F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1 (base cases); F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2) for n >= 2 (recursive case)
What is structural induction used for?	Proving properties of recursively defined structures (like recursively defined sets or trees); mirrors the recursive definition itself, proving the base case then that the property is preserved by the recursive construction rules
Why must every recursive definition include a base case?	Without a base case that doesn't recurse further, the recursion never terminates (infinite recursion)
What proof technique is typically used to prove a recursive algorithm is correct?	(Strong) induction on the size of the input
